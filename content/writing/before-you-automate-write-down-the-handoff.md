---
title: Before you automate, write down the handoff
date: '2026-09-18'
description: 'Map how work moves between people before turning it into a workflow. Ownership, exceptions, and a clear finish matter as much as the trigger.'
---

A task can look repetitive from a distance and become surprisingly complicated
when followed from beginning to end. Copy a record. Send a reminder. Update a
status. Each action seems small, but the handoff between them may depend on a
decision nobody has written down.

Consider a customer enquiry that moves from a sales representative to a
consultation, then to an order and a follow-up. A status change alone does not
explain whether the next person has enough information, whether the customer
needs another conversation, or whether the work has already been completed
somewhere else.

Writing down that movement is a useful first step toward automation.

## Follow one ordinary example

Choose a typical item and trace its journey. Record what starts the work, who
handles it, what information they need, and what they leave for the next person.
Keep the description concrete: "A representative records the agreed callback
time" is more useful than "The system manages engagement."

Then trace an awkward example. What happens when a phone number is missing, an
appointment changes, or a payment needs checking? These cases often reveal the
decisions that a tidy flowchart leaves out.

The result can be a short note or a table. It only needs to make the work clear
enough that somebody else can explain it back.

## Give every handoff an owner

For each transition, answer three questions:

- What makes this item ready to move?
- Who is responsible after it moves?
- What happens if the next step cannot be completed?

A reminder without an owner can become background noise. An automatic assignment
without the necessary context can simply move the confusion to another inbox.
The handoff should carry both responsibility and the information needed to act.

It also helps to define the finish. "Message sent" may be a completed technical
action, while "Customer received an answer" is the outcome the team actually
cares about. Tracking both keeps the workflow honest.

## Automate a bounded step

Once the process is visible, choose a small, predictable part of it. Preparing a
follow-up queue might be a good starting point. Deciding how to handle every
unusual customer situation is a much broader task.

Keep a way to inspect what happened and correct it. A workflow that quietly
repeats a mistake creates more work than a manual step that is easy to check.

The first deliverable may therefore be a clearer process rather than a new
integration. That is useful progress: the automation will inherit whatever
clarity the process already has.
