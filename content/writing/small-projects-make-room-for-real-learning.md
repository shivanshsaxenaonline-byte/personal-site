---
title: Small projects make room for real learning
date: '2026-09-18'
description: 'A narrow project can take you through the whole process: choosing a problem, building a useful path, testing it, and explaining the result.'
---

There is a particular kind of learning that happens when a project reaches
another person. A label that seemed clear needs explaining. An input arrives in
an unexpected form. The most impressive feature turns out to be less useful than
a small detail that was almost left out.

A small project can create those moments sooner. Its value comes from how much
of the process it lets someone finish and understand.

## Choose a problem with an edge

"Build a productivity platform" leaves almost every decision open. "Make a page
that shows which recurring tasks are due today" has a useful boundary. It names
the reader, the information, and the moment when the result matters.

The boundary creates a way to judge progress. Can a reader identify today's work?
Can they tell a completed task from an outstanding one? What should happen when
there are no tasks?

These questions are small enough to answer. They also introduce real design work:
data, presentation, state, and feedback.

## Finish one complete path

A working path connects the beginning of a task to its outcome. Enter a record,
find it again, change it, and understand the result. Even a tiny application
becomes more instructive when that path is complete.

Along the way, give the quiet states some attention. Empty lists, invalid inputs,
and missing information are part of the experience. They are also opportunities
to discover assumptions that the happy path never tests.

Keeping scope narrow makes room for this work. There is time to revisit a label,
remove a confusing control, or explain why an operation failed.

## Keep a short record of decisions

After a change, write down what was confusing, what changed, and how the result
was checked. A few plain sentences are enough.

For example: "The date filter was easy to overlook. It now sits beside the report
title. The report also shows the selected date range." That note captures a
reasoning process that a screenshot alone cannot show.

The record becomes useful when explaining the project later. It gives a more
specific account than a list of technologies: here was the problem, here was a
choice, and here is what the choice made possible.

A project can stay small and still deserve careful work. Finishing, observing,
and explaining one useful thing is a substantial exercise in building software.
