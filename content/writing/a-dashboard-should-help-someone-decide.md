---
title: A dashboard should help someone decide
date: '2026-09-18'
description: 'A useful dashboard connects a number to a question, a decision, and a next step. More charts do not automatically make that connection clearer.'
---

A dashboard earns its place when somebody can look at it and decide what to do
next. That sounds obvious until a page fills with totals, colourful charts,
filters, and comparisons that all compete for attention.

Imagine a team reviewing its daily sales. A large revenue number might be
interesting, but it leaves several questions open. Which period does it cover?
Does it include pending payments? Is the comparison with yesterday meaningful?
What would the team change after reading it?

The useful work begins with those questions.

## Start with one decision

Write a sentence describing the decision the page should support. For example:
"A team lead needs to find follow-ups that require attention today."

That sentence gives the design a centre. The page needs outstanding work,
ownership, and timing. A decorative chart of all-time activity might be less
useful than a short list of overdue tasks with a clear owner beside each one.

Different readers may need different views of the same records. Someone planning
the week needs a pattern over time. Someone handling today's queue needs a
specific item to act on. Trying to satisfy both with the same crowded screen can
make both jobs harder.

## Keep the meaning beside the number

A metric needs enough context to be interpreted. Put the time window, unit, and
definition close to the value. If a figure includes only completed orders, say
so. If the data was last updated this morning, show that too.

An empty value deserves particular care. Zero completed orders and unavailable
data are different situations. Rendering both as a neat zero hides information
the reader needs.

Comparison also needs a fair frame. A partial day beside a full day can suggest a
problem that is simply a difference in the reporting window. A small label can
prevent a large misunderstanding.

## Make the next step reachable

Once a reader notices something, they should be able to investigate it. A summary
can lead to the records behind it. A warning can lead to an owner. A missed target
can lead to the period and definition used to calculate it.

This does not require every chart to become interactive. Sometimes a clear table,
a date filter, and a link to the relevant work are enough.

Before adding another panel, try a simple review: what question does it answer,
who needs that answer, and what can they do with it? If those answers are vague,
the panel probably needs more thought before it needs more polish.
